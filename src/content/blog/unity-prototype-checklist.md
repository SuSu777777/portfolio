---
title: 'A Practical Checklist for Unity Prototypes'
description: 'A short process for testing a game idea before adding polish or content.'
pubDate: 2026-10-23
---

A prototype should answer a question. It does not need to resemble the final game, and it does not need every planned feature. Before opening Unity, write down the uncertainty you want to reduce.

For a movement game, the question might be: *Is moving through one room fun?* For a puzzle game: *Can a new player understand the central rule?* Those questions suggest different prototypes and different tests.

## 1. Define the smallest playable slice

List the actions a player needs to complete one loop. Keep only the input, feedback, and outcome that make the loop understandable. A space game might need a signal, a route, and a scan. It does not yet need a galaxy map, inventory, or skill tree.

## 2. Use temporary art deliberately

Simple shapes are useful when they make mechanics easy to inspect. Give interactive objects distinct colors and states. Temporary art becomes a problem only when it hides collisions, timing, or the meaning of an action.

## 3. Record what happened

After a short playtest, write down three things: what the player tried, where they hesitated, and what they expected to happen. These observations are more useful than asking only whether the game was “fun.”

## 4. Decide what to change

Keep a small decision log. State the problem, the change you will try, and the signal that would show improvement. If a prototype does not answer its original question, narrow it further.

The goal is not to prove that an idea is good. The goal is to learn enough to decide what to build next.
